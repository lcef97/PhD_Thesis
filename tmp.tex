\section{PC-prior derivation in M-models} \label{section:Unpublished}

In this section, we derive the PC-prior for the parameters controlling spatial dependence in the LCAR, PCAR and BYM2 models in the most general and flexible formulation, namely M-models. This general proof goes beyond the block-factorisable structure discussed in this chapter, in which the parameter is univariate, and while the univariate BYM2 case has already been already covered \citep{BYM2, PC}, the univariate case for PCAR and LCAR follows easily by deduction. 

To overcome the difficulties in deriving analytically a PC-prior for a multivariate parameter $\Psi$ of dimension $n_\Psi>1$, we factorise the joint prior using the chain rule, and for convenience we choose the same conditioning value, say $\psi_0$, for all conditionals:
$$
\pi(\Psi) = \pi(\psi_1 \vert \psi_2 =  \dots = \psi_{n_\Psi} = \psi_0) \,
\pi(\psi_2 \vert \psi_3 = \dots = \psi_{n_\Psi} = \psi_0) \, 
\pi(\psi_{n_\Psi-1} \vert \psi_{n_\Psi}=\psi_0)
$$
By doing so, we are employing a stepwise conditioning: at each $j$-th step, we allow an additional parameter $\psi_j$ to vary in the model, and test the flexible model $\mathcal{M}_j\left(z\vert\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{j-1}, \psi_j, \psi_{j+1}=\dots=\psi_{n_\Psi} = \psi_0\right)$ against the base model $\mathcal{M}_{j-1}\left(z\vert\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{j-1}, \psi_j = \psi_{j+1}=\dots=\psi_{n_\Psi} = \psi_0\right)$. For brevity, in the remainder of this section the flexible model proposed at a generic $j$-th step will be simply labelled as $\mathcal{M}_j$.

In the context of PCAR, LCAR, and BYM models, the base model is the non-spatial one, in which either $\boldsymbol{\rho}, \boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ or $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ are matrices of zeroes. Therefore, we will always consider $\psi_0 = 0$ in this section.

Recalling the variance structure of M-models, if $n$ denotes the number of sites, we have 
$$
\mathbb{VAR}[z \vert \Psi] = \left( \boldsymbol{M^\top} \otimes \boldsymbol{I_n} \right) \boldsymbol{\Omega} \left( \boldsymbol{M} \otimes \boldsymbol{I_n} \right) 
$$
Where only the matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ depends on either $\boldsymbol{\rho}, \boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ or $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$. Therefore, at each $j$-th step, the models we compare through the distance function only differ through $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. Specifically, at step $j$ we compare $\mathcal{M}_j$, which assumes $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Omega_j}$ and $\mathcal{M}_{j-1}$ which assumes $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j-1)}}$. 

Since this comparison regards two zero-mean Gaussian densities, the KLD reduces to the simple form
$$
\mathrm{KLD}(\mathcal{M}_j \lvert \rvert \mathcal{M}_{j-1}) = 
-\frac{1}{2} \ln \lvert \boldsymbol{\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1} \mathrm{Var}_{j}}  \rvert
- \frac{kn}{2}
+ \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{tr}\left( \boldsymbol{\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1} \mathrm{Var}_{j}} \right)
$$
Where $\mathrm{Var}_j = \left( \boldsymbol{M^\top} \otimes \boldsymbol{I_n} \right) \boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} \left( \boldsymbol{M} \otimes \boldsymbol{I_n} \right) $ for each $j \in \lbrace 1, \dots, k\rbrace$. From this expression, it is crucial to notice that we do not actually need to know the matrix $\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1} \mathrm{Var}_{j}$, but only its trace and determinant. For brevity, we will refer to this quantity as $\mathrm{KLD}_j$.

Moreover, consider that for two full-rank square matrices $\boldsymbol{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{B}$, matrices $\boldsymbol{AB}$ and $\boldsymbol{BA}$ share the same nonzero eigenvalues. Then, we can notice that $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1} \mathrm{Var}_{j}}$ shares the same eigenvalues of ${\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j-1)}}}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}}$ (equivalently, this is a pair of similar matrices), so only the trace and determinant of this latter matrix need to be known to compute the KLD.

In the context of INLA, requiring several computation of hyperparameter posterior, which in turn requires to compute the prior as we have seen in section \ref{subsection:INLA_int} we need the computational cost of the PC-prior to be negligible; otherwise, this distribution would be of no practical use. On the other hand, computing the eigenvalues of the dense matrix of ${\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j-1)}}}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}}$ is in $\Theta \left( (kn)^3\right)$. For each model, we thus need the KLD to be available in closed formula, but also to require a low computational cost, by leveraging on the properties of the model-specific $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ matrices. Both these conditions hold for the univariate BYM2 mixing parameter \citep{BYM2, PC}. We will see that this is also the case of the models in scope. Moreover, the KLD corresponding to a single parameter is independent on all other parameters, implying that the conditional prior reduces to the relevant marginal. Taking the PCAR as an example, we have thus that
$$
\pi(\boldsymbol{\rho}) = \pi(\rho_1)\, \pi(\rho_2)\,\dots \,  \pi(\rho_k)
$$

\subsection{PCAR Model}
We start with the PCAR. Here, at the $j$-th step, we compare the models:
$$
\mathcal{M}_j: \boldsymbol{\rho} = \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_{j-1}, \rho_j, 0, \dots, 0 \right)
$$
Against
$$
\mathcal{M}_{j-1}: \boldsymbol{\rho} = \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\rho_1, \dots, \rho_{j-1},0, \dots, 0 \right)
$$

We assume that the graph degree matrix $\boldsymbol{D}$ is invertible. Considering the alternative models above, we can see that the KLD for the $j$-th step is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:KLD_pcar}
\mathrm{KLD}_j =  \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{1-\rho_j h_i}
- \ln \frac{1}{1 - \rho_j h_i}- 1\right)
\end{equation}
Where $\boldsymbol{H} = \mathrm{diag}\left(h_1, h_2, \dots, h_{n}\right)$ are the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}}$, equal to the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}$, which as we have briefly mentioned in section \ref{subsection:pcar} are all inside the unit disk. Hence, for $\rho \in [0,1[$, the summands are non-negative\footnote{they are of the form $x-\ln x-1$ whose minimum is $0$ at $x=1$}, the KLD is monotonically non-decreasing and it ranges from $0$ to $\infty$. Moreover, the derivative is:
$$
\frac{d\mathrm{KLD}_j}{d \rho_j} =  -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n  \frac{h_i}{1 - \rho_j h_i} +
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{h_i}{(1-\rho_j h_i)^2}
= \frac{\rho_j}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{ h_i^2}{(1-\rho_j h_i)^2}
$$
which for $\rho_j \in ]0,1[$ is always strictly positive.

Importantly, the matrix $\boldsymbol{D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}}$ is deterministic, and its eigenvalues only need to be determined once. Hence the calculation of $\pi(\boldsymbol{\rho})$ is scalable.


\subsection{LCAR Model}
For the LCAR model at the $j$-th step, we compare the models: 
$$\mathcal{M}_j: \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda^{(j)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{j-1}, \lambda_j, 0, \dots, 0 \right)$$
Against 
$$\mathcal{M}_{j-1}: \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda^{(j-1)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{j-1},0, \dots, 0 \right)$$
In this case, the KLD reads as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KLD}_j = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(
\frac{1}{1+\lambda_j (\ell_i-1)} - \ln \frac{1}{1+\lambda_j (\ell_i-1)} - 1 \right)
\label{eq:KLD_LCAR}
\end{equation}
Where $\lbrace \ell_j \rbrace_{i=1}^n$ are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix. In analogy with the PCAR model, we can see that the summands are non-negative, and the KLD is a monotonically non-decreasing function of $\lambda_j$, ranging from 0 (with $\lambda_j=0$) to $\infty$. With some algebra we can see that the derivative is
$$
\frac{d \mathrm{KLD}_j}{d \lambda_j} = \frac{\lambda_j}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{ (\ell_i - 1)^2}{(1+\lambda_j (\ell_i - 1) )^2}
$$
As in the previous case, the Laplacian eigenvalues are deterministic and must be computed once, hence this function is scalable in computational terms.

\subsection{BYM2 model}
Lastly, for the BYM2 model at each step we compare:
$$\mathcal{M}_j: \boldsymbol{\Phi} = \boldsymbol{\Phi^{(j)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_{j-1}, \phi_j, 0, \dots, 0 \right)$$
Against 
$$\mathcal{M}_{j-1}: \boldsymbol{\Phi} = \boldsymbol{\Phi^{(j-1)}} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_{j-1},0, \dots, 0 \right)$$
We then obtain the KLD as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{KLD}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n-G} \left[
\ln\left( 1 -\phi_j \frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*}\right)
-\phi_j \left(\frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*} \right)
\right] + G\left[ -\ln(1-\phi_j) - \phi_j\right]
\end{equation}
Which equals the univariate case already covered by \cite{BYM2} and \cite{PC}. Therefore, for the BYM2, we have simply shown that using a stepwise procedure to define the multivariate PC-prior, it reduces to the product of univariate marginals, which are already a known functional form.




\section{Detail of KLD calculations}
In this section, we prove the results of the KLD for the parameters of spatial dependence of the models in scope. In this appendix, we use the $1_n$ notation for a vector of ones of length $n$.
\subsection{PCAR}
In this case, we have
$$
\boldsymbol{{\Omega^{(j-1)}}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} = 
\left( \boldsymbol{I_k} \otimes \boldsymbol{D} \right)
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right)
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right)^{-1}
\left( \boldsymbol{I_k} \otimes \boldsymbol{D} \right)^{-1}
$$
This matrix has the same eigenvalues of $\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right)
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right)^{-1}$. Let us define $\boldsymbol{\chi}$ as the eigenvector matrix of $\boldsymbol{D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}}$, and $\boldsymbol{H}$ as its eigenvalues. Then, we have
$$
\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W} = 
\left( \boldsymbol{I_k} \otimes\boldsymbol{D^{-1/2}\chi}\right)
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}} \otimes \boldsymbol{H} \right)
\left( \boldsymbol{I_k} \otimes\boldsymbol{D^{-1/2}\chi}\right)^{-1}
$$
This matrix can be seen to commute with $\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right)^{-1}$. Therefore, the eigenvalue matrix of $\boldsymbol{{\Omega^{(j-1)}}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} $ is given by $\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}} \otimes \boldsymbol{H} \right)\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}} \otimes \boldsymbol{H} \right)^{-1}$. Consider that, for each $j$, factors of the eigenvalue matrix are thus structured:
$$
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}}\otimes \boldsymbol{D^{-1}W}\right) =
\left( \begin{array}{cccc:c}
\left(\boldsymbol{I_n} - \rho_1\boldsymbol{H}\right) 
& \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}}& \left(\boldsymbol{I_n} - \rho_2\boldsymbol{H}\right) 
& \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \left(\boldsymbol{I_n} - \rho_j\boldsymbol{H}\right) & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\hdashline
\boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{I_{(k-j)n}}
\end{array} \right)
$$
Therefore, the eigenvalue matrix we are looking for will read as follows:
\begin{align*}
\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j-1)}} \otimes \boldsymbol{H} \right)\left(\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} - \boldsymbol{\rho^{(j)}} \otimes \boldsymbol{H} \right)^{-1} = \\ =
\left( \begin{array}{cccc:c}
\boldsymbol{I_n} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}}& \boldsymbol{I_n} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \left(\boldsymbol{I_n} - \rho_j\boldsymbol{H}\right)^{-1} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\hdashline
\boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{I_{(k-j)n}}
\end{array} \right)
\end{align*}
It is easy to see that these eigenvalues do not depend on any other value of $\boldsymbol{\rho}$ except from $\rho_j$. Hence the KLD for $\rho_j$ will be independent on other autocorrelation parameters. 
To sum up, we can notice that for the determinant in the KLD formula we have
$$
\lvert\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1}\mathrm{Var}_j} \rvert = 
\prod_{i=1}^n  \Big{\{ }\prod_{r=1}^{j} (1 - \rho_r h_i)^{-1} \prod_{r=1}^{j-1} (1 - \rho_r h_i) \Big{ \}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} (1 - \rho_j h_i)^{-1}
$$

For the trace, we have instead:
$$
\mathrm{tr}\left( \boldsymbol{\mathrm{Var}_{j-1}^{-1}\mathrm{Var}_j} \right)= (k-1)n + \sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{1}{1- \rho_j h_i}
$$
Therefore, the KLD reads as in equation \ref{eq:KLD_pcar}. For $\rho_j=0$ the KLD is clearly zero, as the distance between two identical models is zero by definition. 

\subsection{LCAR}

For convenience, the between-fields spatial variance-covariance matrix can be rewritten, at each step $j$, as $\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} = \left[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda_j} \otimes\left( \boldsymbol{L} - \boldsymbol{I_n} \right)+\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} \right]^{-1}$. Then, we can notice that
\begin{align*}
\boldsymbol{{\Omega^{(j-1)}}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} = \\ =
\left( \begin{array}{cccc:c}
\boldsymbol{I_n} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}}& \boldsymbol{I_n} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \left[\lambda_j\left( \boldsymbol{L}-\boldsymbol{I_n} \right) + \boldsymbol{I_n}\right]^{-1} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\hdashline
\boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{I_{(k-j)n}}
\end{array} \right)
\end{align*}
The eigenvalues of this matrix are
$$
\left(
1_{(j-1)n}, \frac{1}{1+\lambda_j (\ell_1-1)}, \dots , \frac{1}{1+\lambda_j (\ell_n-1)}, 1_{(p-j)n}
\right)
$$
Where $\lbrace \ell_j \rbrace_{i=1}^n$ are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix; recalling the Laplacian matrix properties, these eigenvalues are all non-negative, and the number of zero eigenvalues is given by the number of graph connected components. Therefore, it is easy to see that the KLD then reads as in equation \ref{eq:KLD_LCAR}

\subsection{BYM2}

In analogy with the LCAR, the between-fields spatial variance-covariance matrix can conveniently be rewritten, at each step $j$, as $\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} =  \boldsymbol{\Phi_j} \otimes\left( \boldsymbol{L}_s^+ - \boldsymbol{I_n} \right)+\boldsymbol{I_{kn}} $, where $\boldsymbol{L_s}$ is the scaled Laplacian. In this case, we can see that
\begin{align*}
\boldsymbol{{\Omega^{(j-1)}}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}} = \\ =
\left( \begin{array}{cccc:c}
\boldsymbol{I_n} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}}& \boldsymbol{I_n} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{n \times n}} & \dots & \phi_j\left( \boldsymbol{L_s^+}-\boldsymbol{I_n} \right) + \boldsymbol{I_n} & \boldsymbol{0_{n\times(k-j)n}} \\
\hdashline
\boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \dots & \boldsymbol{0_{(k-j)n\times n}} & \boldsymbol{I_{(k-j)n}}
\end{array} \right)
\end{align*}
This formula includes the eigenvalues of the (scaled) pseudo-inverse of the Laplacian. Let us denote $\lbrace\ell_i^*\rbrace_{i=1}^{n}$ the eigenvalues of the scaled Laplacian and, with no loss of generality, order them decreasingly. By doing so, we have $\ell_i^* > 0 \quad \forall i \in \lbrace1, \dots, n-G\rbrace$ and $\ell_i^* = 0 \quad \forall i \in \lbrace n-G+1, \dots, n\rbrace$, where $G$ are the graph connected components. Now, the first $n-G$ eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{L_s^+}$ are simply given by $\lbrace 1/ \ell_i^* \rbrace_{i=1}^{n-G}$, while the last $G$ eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{L_s^+}$ are zeroes. Now, the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{{\Omega^{(j-1)}}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\Omega^{(j)}}$ matrix are given by
\begin{equation}
\left(
1_{(j-1)n}, 1-\phi_j \frac{\ell_1^*-1}{\ell_1^*},  \dots,1-\phi_j \frac{\ell_{n-G}^*-1}{\ell_{n-G}^*},
(1-\phi_j)1_G, 1_{(p-j)n}
\right)
\label{eq:eigen_KLD_BYM}
\end{equation}
We can see that the eigenvalues depending on Laplacian eigenvalues are all positive if $\psi_j \in ]0,1[$. For any $i \in \lbrace1, \dots, n-G\rbrace$ let us consider three cases:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\ell_i \in \left]0,1 \right[$. In this case, $\frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*}$ is positive and therefore $-\phi_j \frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*} > 1$.
\item $\ell_i = 1$ In this case, $1-\phi_j \frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*} = 1$.
\item $\ell_i > 1$ In this case, $\frac{\ell_1^*-1}{\ell_1^*} \in \, ]0, 1[$, hence $1-\phi_j \frac{\ell_i^*-1}{\ell_i^*}  \in ]0, 1[$
\end{enumerate}
In equation \ref{eq:eigen_KLD_BYM}, $(k-1)n$ eigenvalues are ones; as a consequence, their contribution to the KLD is zero. Therefore, we obtain a formula for the $\mathrm{KLD}(\phi_j)$ equivalent to the univariate case covered by \cite{BYM2} in Appendix A2.2 or \cite{PC} in Appendix A.2
